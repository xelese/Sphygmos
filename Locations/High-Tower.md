# High Tower - Bastion

![The High Tower](https://s3.us-east-2.wasabisys.com/campaign-sphygmos-public-images/Locations/High%20Tower/high-tower.png)


> *The High Tower stands as a beacon of knowledge and authority. Its spires reach into the sky, symbolizing the pursuit of understanding and control over the natural and magical world. The surrounding area is meticulously maintained, reflecting the Tower's influence and the discipline of its inhabitants.*

## History

The High Tower was once the foremost academy of the known world — a sprawling campus of lecture halls, libraries, dormitories, and research wings devoted to every branch of scholarship. Students came from distant regions to study natural philosophy, arcane theory, medicine, engineering, history, and cartography. Its name carried weight the way great institutions do: not through force, but through the assumption that anyone worth knowing had trained or studied there.

When the Vital Gradient began to fail, the Tower pivoted. Admissions froze. Curricula were abandoned. Faculty who studied ancient history or theoretical cosmology found themselves reassigned to field observation, collapse modeling, and emergency triage. Within weeks, the campus stopped being a university and became a command center.

Now, brilliant minds arrive not to earn degrees but to solve a crisis. The campus expands not through enrollment but through urgency — new wings, facilities, and workshops are built as the problem demands and resources allow. The Tower still carries the architecture and atmosphere of a great academy, but its purpose has narrowed to a single question: how to stop the world from dying.

## Wings

### Quartermaster's Wing

The High Tower supply bay feels less like a shop and more like a disciplined machine that happens to sell things.


The room is tall, narrow, and crowded upward rather than outward, with shelves climbing into shadow and a brass rail system fixed along the rafters like the track of some patient industrial beast. Warm lanternlight hangs in pockets through the space, catching on metal edges, bottle glass, and polished instrument housings, so that every surface seems to glow faintly with use. Nothing is luxurious. Everything is maintained.


The counter is a long slab of scarred wood darkened by oil, soot, ink, and years of hard handling. Behind it, ranked drawers cover the walls in neat banks, each one labeled in a careful institutional hand: assay salts, rod fittings, lens cloth, wick caps, binding wire, chalk tabs, tower seals. Some drawers sit flush and tidy. Others are slightly ajar, as if opened and shut a hundred times a day by quick mechanical hands. Stacked around the counter are reinforced cases with brass corners, bundles of rope, sealed crates, hooked climbing rigs, and squat iron lanterns waiting to be issued to another survey team heading somewhere unwise.


Overall, the shop feels procedural, severe, and dependable. This is where expeditions are measured, not inspired. People come here before going into dangerous places. They leave with rope, instruments, sealed cases, warnings, and the uncomfortable sense that the Tower has already calculated the odds.


T.H.R.U.S.H resides here.

### Research Wing

The Research Wing feels less like a library and more like a weather system built for thought. The air is cool, thin with dust, oil, and the faint mineral scent of worked crystal, and every sound seems to arrive already reduced to its useful parts: the scratch of a quill, the click of a lens rotating into place, the low hum of apparatus left running because interruption is risk.


The chamber architecture is lofty but compressed by function. Narrow balconies ring higher levels in stacked tiers, their railings crowded with notebooks, specimen trays, brass instruments, and suspended measuring frames that can be lowered by chain and pulley. Light enters reluctantly through tall slit windows and is caught, split, and redirected by mirrored plates and crystal vanes, so the room never feels fully sunlit. Instead it glows in controlled bands and pale reflections, as if illumination itself has been cataloged.


Long tables of dark wood and black stone dominate the floor, each scarred by heat, acid, ink, and repeated disassembly. Their surfaces are crowded without being chaotic: pinned diagrams, glass cylinders, calibration weights, trays of labeled fragments, and articulated stands holding objects at exact angles for observation. The walls are not decorative. They are occupied. Shelving climbs almost to the rafters, dense with sealed archives, field ledgers, boxed samples, and instrument cases marked in the tidy, severe hand of the Tower. Nothing here invites touch casually. Everything seems to assume that if you are handling it, you are being judged by it.


What makes the wing unsettling is not danger but discipline. Scholars move through it with the hush and certainty of people accustomed to treating wonder as workload. Assistants carry slates and lamps from station to station. Archivists confer in low voices beside hanging charts and wheeled star maps. Even fatigue has been standardized into posture and routine. The place conveys a clear philosophy: knowledge is not discovered here so much as extracted, stabilized, and entered into record before uncertainty can reclaim it.


Overall, the Research Wing feels brilliant, exacting, and slightly inhuman. It is where the Tower turns observation into authority. People enter with questions, fragments, or fear, and leave with classifications, models, cautions, and the unnerving impression that whatever they brought in has been made smaller and more precise than it was in the world outside.
